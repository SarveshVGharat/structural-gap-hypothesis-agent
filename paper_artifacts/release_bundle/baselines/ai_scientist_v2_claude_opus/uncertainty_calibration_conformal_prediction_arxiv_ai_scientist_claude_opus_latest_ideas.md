## 1. Self-Fulfilling Coverage: How Prediction Sets Corrupt the Labels That Calibrate Them

### Name

self_fulfilling_conformal_coverage

### Short Hypothesis

When conformal prediction sets are shown to the annotators (humans or LLMs) whose labels later serve as calibration data, the labels become endogenous to the sets: annotators anchor on displayed candidates, so observed coverage stays at 1-alpha while true coverage collapses and sets shrink toward a degenerate fixed point. We hypothesize (i) this performative loop is provably self-fulfilling above an anchoring threshold, (ii) it occurs empirically with real LLM annotators, and (iii) it can be detected and corrected either by a small fraction of set-blind audit labels or by randomizing the displayed nominal level as an instrument that identifies anchoring strength.

### Related Work

Conformal prediction (Vovk et al.; Angelopoulos & Bates 2021) assumes exchangeable, exogenous calibration labels. Robustness to label noise (Einbinder et al. 2022; Sesia et al. 2023; Penso & Goldberger 2024) assumes the noise process is independent of the prediction sets; here noise is *caused* by the sets, i.e., endogenous and self-reinforcing. Adaptive/online CP (Gibbs & Candes 2021, 2022) and CP under covariate/label shift (Tibshirani et al. 2019; Barber et al. 2023) still treat labels as ground truth. Conformal under 'feedback covariate shift' (Fannjiang et al. 2022) lets the model choose which x to query but keeps y exogenous. Performative prediction (Perdomo et al. 2020) studies how deployed point predictions shift the feature/label distribution but does not analyze distribution-free coverage guarantees or their self-fulfilment. Human-AI studies of prediction sets (Cresswell et al. 2024; Babbar et al. 2022; Steyvers et al. 2022; Zhang et al. on anchoring to AI advice) document that displayed sets change human answers and can induce over-reliance, but measure decision accuracy, not the downstream consequence for the validity of recalibration. To our knowledge no work formalizes or fixes the closed loop 'sets -> anchored labels -> recalibration -> smaller sets', which is exactly the loop in deployed continual-calibration pipelines (medical triage, content moderation, LLM-assisted annotation).

### Abstract

Conformal prediction promises distribution-free coverage, provided calibration labels are drawn independently of the prediction sets. In deployment this assumption is routinely broken: prediction sets are displayed to the humans or LLM annotators whose answers are later recycled as calibration labels. We show that this creates a performative feedback loop in which coverage becomes self-fulfilling. We model an anchored annotator whose reported label falls inside the displayed set with a probability that increases as the set shrinks, and analyze the resulting recursion on the conformal quantile. We prove that above an anchoring threshold depending only on the anchoring strength and alpha, the recursion has a unique fixed point at which measured coverage equals the nominal 1-alpha while true coverage is strictly and quantifiably lower, and sets contract to (near-)singletons; below the threshold the loop is stable and coverage is retained. We then demonstrate the loop empirically in three settings: (1) simulated anchoring on ImageNet/CIFAR-100 with APS/RAPS scores, (2) anchoring behaviour fit to public human-with-AI labelling datasets, and (3) a fully real closed loop in which an LLM annotator is shown prediction sets on multiple-choice QA and its answers are fed back into recalibration, yielding a 20+ point gap between reported and true coverage within a few rounds. Finally we give two remedies with guarantees: epsilon-blind auditing, where a small randomized fraction of set-withheld labels restores finite-sample validity at a characterized efficiency cost, and level randomization, where jittering the displayed alpha acts as an instrument that identifies the anchoring function and de-biases coverage estimates without any blind labels.

### Experiments

1. Theory and its simulation. Model: annotator reports Y' = Y with prob 1-rho(|C|), else uniform over C (and variants: rank-based anchoring, refusal, only-anchors-when-Y not in C). Derive the map from calibration quantile q_t to q_{t+1} under score s (APS/RAPS/softmax), prove existence/uniqueness of fixed points and a threshold condition for collapse; validate predicted fixed point (set size, true coverage) against Monte-Carlo simulation on a synthetic Gaussian mixture and on real score distributions from pretrained ResNet/ViT logits on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-val. Metrics: predicted vs observed fixed-point set size and true coverage (gold labels held aside), number of rounds to collapse, phase diagram over (rho, alpha, model accuracy).
2. Grounding anchoring in real human data. Fit rho(|C|) and the within-set choice model on public human-with-AI-assistance label datasets (e.g. ImageNet-16H style human predictions with/without model advice, CIFAR-10H as the no-advice baseline, and released data from prediction-set human studies). Report fitted anchoring strength and plug it into the theory to predict, for each dataset, how many recalibration rounds until the true/reported coverage gap exceeds 5/10/20 points.
3. Real closed loop with LLM annotators (cheap, no human subjects). Base predictor: small model (e.g. fine-tuned Flan-T5 or Llama-3-8B logits) on MMLU / MedMCQA / TruthfulQA-MC. Annotator: a separate LLM (e.g. GPT-4o-mini or Llama-3-70B via API) shown the question plus the current prediction set and asked for the answer, with a 'none of these' option. Run T=10 rounds of split-conformal recalibration using annotator labels; track reported coverage (vs annotator labels), true coverage (vs gold), average set size, anchoring rate as a function of set size, and repeat with set-blind annotation as control. Ablate prompt framing ('these are model candidates' vs no framing) and annotator strength.
4. Mitigation A: epsilon-blind auditing. Route a random epsilon fraction of calibration points to set-free labelling; calibrate on audit points only, and on a variance-optimal convex combination with anchored points de-biased by the estimated anchoring model. Report true coverage, set size, and the epsilon needed to keep true coverage within 1 point of nominal at fixed labelling budget; compare against theory-predicted epsilon.
5. Mitigation B: level randomization as an instrument. Present sets built at a randomized level alpha_i ~ Uniform{alpha-delta,...,alpha+delta} (so displayed set size varies exogenously); estimate rho(|C|) and the anchoring-corrected coverage by regressing observed conformity on set size across the randomized arms; evaluate bias of the recovered rho and of corrected coverage estimates, and the coverage of the resulting corrected prediction sets, in settings 1 and 3. Compare to naive CP, noise-robust CP baselines (Einbinder et al., Sesia et al.), and epsilon-blind auditing.
6. Diagnostic test. Propose a simple sequential test for performative contamination (comparing conformity-score distributions across randomized-level arms or audit vs anchored labels) and report power/type-I error as a function of sample size and rho.

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(a) The anchoring model is a stylized abstraction; real annotators may anchor non-monotonically in set size, which would weaken the collapse theorem (mitigated by testing several anchoring families and fitting to real data). (b) LLM annotators may anchor much more or less strongly than humans, so setting 3 demonstrates the mechanism rather than human magnitudes; we do not run new human subject studies. (c) Public human-with-AI datasets display top-k or single predictions rather than conformal sets, so fitted anchoring parameters are approximations. (d) Level randomization requires the annotator's anchoring to depend on the displayed set only through observable features (an exclusion-restriction-style assumption) which we can test but not guarantee. (e) Epsilon-blind auditing needs the ability to collect some set-free labels, which may be infeasible in fully automated pipelines. (f) Effect sizes may be modest when base models are accurate, so the practical severity of the loop is regime-dependent, and we will map that regime explicitly.

## 2. Leaky Calibration: Data Contamination Silently Invalidates Conformal Prediction

### Name

contaminated_calibration_conformal

### Short Hypothesis

Conformal prediction requires calibration and test points to be exchangeable with respect to the conformity score, but when calibration examples were (partially) seen during pretraining/training, memorization shifts their scores in a strictly favorable, one-sided direction. We hypothesize that this 'leakage shift' causes systematic under-coverage on fresh data that is invisible to standard diagnostics (no covariate shift, no label noise), that its magnitude is predictable from the contamination rate and a measurable memorization gap, and that it can be repaired with membership-weighted or membership-group-conditional calibration at a small efficiency cost.

### Related Work

(1) CP foundations and beyond-exchangeability: Vovk et al.; Angelopoulos & Bates (2021); Tibshirani et al. (2019) weighted CP under covariate shift; Barber et al. (2023) 'Conformal prediction beyond exchangeability' gives coverage bounds in terms of total-variation-like distances but neither identifies training leakage as a source nor exploits its one-sided structure; Gibbs & Candes adaptive CP targets temporal drift, not memorization. Robust CP work addresses label noise (Einbinder et al. 2022; Sesia et al. 2023) and adversarial corruption, i.e., noise in y, not a score shift caused by the predictor having memorized the calibration point. (2) Contamination literature: min-K% Prob (Shi et al. 2023), Oren et al. (2023) 'Proving Test Set Contamination' (which uses exchangeability of example ordering as a *detection* tool), GSM1k (Zhang et al. 2024), LiveBench, benchmark-duplication studies (Barz & Denzler on CIFAR duplicates; Recht et al. new test sets). These measure inflated *accuracy*; none study the consequences for distribution-free *validity* of prediction sets. (3) CP for LLMs: conformal MCQ sets (Kumar et al. 2023; Ye et al. 2024), conformal factuality (Mohri & Hashimoto 2024), conformal language modeling (Quach et al. 2024) — all calibrate on public benchmarks that are plausibly contaminated, so our result directly questions the transferability of their guarantees. Our contribution is the missing link: a formalization of leakage as a directional score shift, an identification strategy that separates memorization from temporal distribution shift, measurements with verified pretraining-corpus membership (infini-gram over Dolma for OLMo), and simple valid remedies.

### Abstract

Conformal prediction is advertised as assumption-light: given exchangeable calibration data, prediction sets attain 1-alpha coverage regardless of the model. We point out a widespread and previously unexamined violation of this premise. Modern predictors — especially LLMs calibrated on public benchmarks — have often already seen part of their calibration data during training. Memorization makes conformity scores on such points anomalously good, in a strictly one-sided way, so the calibrated quantile is too small and the resulting sets under-cover fresh data, while every standard diagnostic (covariate distributions, label quality, split-conformal coverage measured on the same benchmark) looks fine. We formalize leakage as a mixture score shift with a contamination rate rho and a memorization gap g, and derive a tight one-sided lower bound on test coverage that shows how the coverage deficit grows with rho*g and shrinks with calibration-set size; symmetrically, we show that evaluating coverage on a contaminated test split produces a 'coverage illusion' that hides the loss. Empirically, we (i) validate the theory in a controlled setting by fine-tuning small models on a known subset of the calibration pool and sweeping rho; (ii) measure real leakage shift in vision using train/calibration near-duplicates; (iii) measure it in LLMs using verified pretraining-corpus membership via public n-gram indices, and using a temporal-split design with a placebo model whose cutoff postdates both splits, which identifies the memorization component separately from genuine distribution shift. Finally we give three remedies — membership-weighted conformal prediction, membership-group-conditional (Mondrian) calibration, and worst-case alpha inflation — and quantify the efficiency price of restoring validity.

### Experiments

1. Theory. Model the calibration score distribution as (1-rho)F_clean + rho*F_mem where F_mem stochastically dominates F_clean by a gap g (e.g., quantile shift). Derive (a) the exact asymptotic test coverage as F_clean^{-1} evaluated at the contaminated quantile, (b) a finite-sample one-sided lower bound, (c) the size of the 'coverage illusion' when the test split is itself contaminated at rate rho', and (d) the alpha-inflation needed for worst-case validity given a bound on rho. Verify all formulas by Monte Carlo with real score distributions taken from pretrained model logits.
2. Controlled leakage (clean causal knob). Vision: train ResNet-18/ViT-tiny on CIFAR-100 with an extra 'leaked' subset L of the calibration pool included in training for k in {0,1,5,20} extra epochs; language: continue-pretrain Pythia-410M/1.4B on a leaked subset of an MCQA dataset. Sweep rho in {0,0.05,0.1,0.25,0.5} and memorization strength k. Report: coverage on clean held-out test, average set size, measured memorization gap g, and predicted-vs-observed coverage from the theory. Scores: softmax/APS/RAPS and LLM logit-margin scores.
3. Naturally occurring leakage in vision. Detect near-duplicates between ImageNet/CIFAR training sets and the standard validation pool using CLIP/SSCD embedding retrieval plus manual verification of a sample. Split the calibration pool into duplicate vs non-duplicate; calibrate on each and evaluate on a de-duplicated held-out set (plus on new test sets such as ImageNetV2 / CIFAR-10.1). Metric: coverage gap and set-size gap between duplicate-calibrated and clean-calibrated CP at matched calibration size.
4. Verified LLM contamination. Use OLMo-7B (and Pythia) whose pretraining corpora (Dolma, the Pile) are queryable via the public infini-gram API: for MCQA benchmarks (MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MedMCQA) label each calibration item as contaminated/clean by long-n-gram membership in the corpus; also compute continuous membership scores (min-K% Prob). Calibrate CP on contaminated-only, clean-only, and mixed pools; evaluate coverage on clean items. Report coverage vs contamination rate curves and correlation between membership score and conformity score (the empirical 'memorization gap').
5. Temporal split with a placebo model (identification). For benchmarks with time-stamped items (LiveBench, new exam sets, GSM8K vs GSM1k, post-cutoff news QA): calibrate on pre-cutoff items, test on post-cutoff items, and compare with random-split calibration. Repeat with a 'placebo' model whose training cutoff postdates both splits (both splits equally unseen) and with a model whose cutoff predates both (both seen). If the coverage gap shrinks for the placebo model, the gap is attributable to memorization rather than temporal distribution shift; report this difference-in-differences estimate of the leakage-induced coverage loss.
6. Remedies and their cost. (a) Membership-weighted CP: treat contamination as a shift and use weighted conformal quantiles with weights derived from a calibrated membership classifier (min-K% Prob / n-gram evidence), i.e., down-weight likely-memorized calibration points; (b) Mondrian CP conditional on membership group, applied with the clean group's quantile; (c) worst-case alpha inflation using an upper bound on rho from a contamination detector. Compare against vanilla split CP, Barber-et-al-style robust CP, and oracle calibration on verified-clean data. Metrics: marginal coverage on clean/fresh data, group-conditional coverage, mean set size (efficiency cost of validity), and sensitivity to membership-detector error.
7. Audit test. A one-sided two-sample test (e.g. Mann-Whitney on conformity scores of high- vs low-membership calibration points) that certifies absence of leakage shift; report type-I error and power vs (rho, g, n) in settings 2 and 4, and run it as a diagnostic on popular CP-for-LLM setups.

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(a) Memorization may inflate accuracy without much changing conformity-score ranks, so the coverage loss could be small on some benchmark/model pairs; the controlled experiments will map exactly when the effect matters. (b) Contamination labels are imperfect: n-gram membership misses paraphrased leakage and min-K% Prob is a noisy detector, which will attenuate measured gaps (we mitigate with verified-corpus models and manual audits). (c) The temporal design confounds memorization with genuine distribution shift; the placebo-model difference-in-differences mitigates but does not fully eliminate this (models differ in more than cutoff). (d) Membership-weighted CP requires an assumption that leakage affects scores only through the observed membership variable; we can test this but not guarantee it. (e) Vision near-duplicate detection has false positives/negatives. (f) Only open-weight models with public corpora allow gold-standard membership, limiting how frontier-model conclusions can be stated. (g) We do not pretrain large models; conclusions about scale-dependence of memorization shift are extrapolations from small controlled models plus observational open-weight evidence.

## 3. Coverage-on-the-Line: Estimating and Restoring Conformal Coverage Under Distribution Shift Without Any Target Labels

### Name

coverage_on_the_line

### Short Hypothesis

Conformal coverage on shifted data can be estimated, monitored, and restored using only unlabeled target data, because a purely label-free quantity -- the rate at which one model's prediction falls inside another model's conformal set ('pseudo-coverage') -- is strongly linearly related, across model pairs, to the unobservable true coverage; whenever this line is observed to hold, solving for the calibration level that hits nominal predicted coverage yields valid sets under shift with no target labels and no covariate-shift/likelihood-ratio assumptions.

### Related Work

Weighted conformal prediction (Tibshirani et al. 2019) restores coverage under covariate shift but needs likelihood ratios and fails under label/concept shift; Barber et al. (2023) give beyond-exchangeability bounds that depend on unknown distributional distances; adaptive/online CP (Gibbs & Candes 2021, 2022) and PID/ACI variants require *streaming target labels* to react to shift; conformal risk control and JAWS/ RLSCP likewise consume labeled target data or assume a shift model. Robust-CP work targets label noise or adversarial perturbations, not label-free coverage estimation. On the other side, the label-free performance-estimation literature -- accuracy-on-the-line (Miller et al. 2021), agreement-on-the-line (Baek et al. 2022), ATC (Garg et al. 2022), DoC, ensemble-disagreement estimators (Jiang et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2021) -- predicts *point accuracy or calibration error*, never the coverage of prediction sets, and provides no mechanism to recalibrate a conformal predictor. Prior CP-under-shift papers that use unlabeled target data only estimate density ratios; none exploit inter-model agreement structure. Our proposal is the missing bridge: a set-valued analogue of agreement-on-the-line that (i) yields the first practical label-free conformal coverage monitor, (ii) turns it into a target-label-free recalibration rule with an accompanying identifiability theory and explicit failure diagnostics, and (iii) characterizes exactly which shift types break it (label shift, spurious correlations, correlated model zoos).

### Abstract

Conformal prediction guarantees coverage only under exchangeability, so its guarantee silently evaporates under distribution shift -- and detecting this requires labeled target data, which is exactly what is unavailable at deployment. We ask a simple question: can coverage be estimated without labels? We observe that replacing the missing target label with the prediction of an *independently trained* second model gives a fully label-free statistic, the pseudo-coverage, i.e. the fraction of target points where model B's prediction lies inside model A's conformal set. Pseudo-coverage is biased by B's error, but we show that its bias is highly structured: across many model pairs it traces a tight linear relationship between in-distribution and out-of-distribution pseudo-coverage whose slope and intercept closely match those of true ID-vs-OOD coverage, a phenomenon we call coverage-on-the-line. We prove that the bias equals a difference of two asymmetric error terms that cancel exactly under a conditional-error-symmetry condition, and that a probit-scaling shift model implies the observed linearity. Empirically, on model zoos over CIFAR-10/100-C, ImageNet-C/R/V2/Sketch, WILDS, and multiple-choice-QA shifts for LLMs, our estimator predicts OOD coverage of APS/RAPS/THR sets to within a few points, far better than nominal, ATC-, confidence-, and importance-weighting-based baselines. We then close the loop: choosing the source calibration level so that predicted target coverage equals 1-alpha yields target-label-free prediction sets that recover nominal coverage at a modest size cost relative to an oracle calibrated on labeled target data. Finally we map when the method fails -- label shift, spurious-feature shift, and highly correlated model zoos -- and give a label-free diagnostic that flags these cases.

### Experiments

1. Model zoos and setup. Vision: (a) train ~40 CIFAR-10/100 models varying architecture (ResNet/VGG/ViT-tiny/MLP-mixer), seed, augmentation, epochs; (b) use ~50 pretrained ImageNet models from timm. Shifts: CIFAR-10-C/100-C (all 15 corruptions x 5 severities), CIFAR-10.1/10.2, ImageNet-C/R/V2/Sketch/A, WILDS Camelyon17, iWildCam, FMoW. Conformity scores: THR (softmax), APS, RAPS; alpha in {0.05, 0.1, 0.2}. Split-conformal calibration on held-out ID labeled data.
2. Measure the phenomenon. For every ordered model pair (A,B): compute ID and OOD true coverage of A's sets, and ID and OOD pseudo-coverage (rate at which B's top-1 prediction lies in A's set); also a symmetrized variant and a score-based variant (conformity score of A evaluated at B's pseudo-label). Plot OOD-vs-ID for coverage and for pseudo-coverage on probit axes; report R^2, slope, intercept, and slope/intercept mismatch between the two lines, per shift and per score function.
3. Coverage estimators. Build (a) CAline-S: fit the pseudo-coverage line over model pairs, assume the coverage line shares its slope/intercept, and map observed ID coverage (approximately 1-alpha) to predicted OOD coverage; (b) CAline-D: solve for the coverage value that makes the two lines coincide; (c) a simple debiased pairwise estimator using the theoretical bias decomposition with an ATC-style estimate of model B's OOD error. Baselines: nominal 1-alpha; ATC/DoC applied to conformity scores; single-model self-consistency (augmentation-invariance) estimate; importance-weighted CP with domain-classifier weights (Tibshirani et al.); ensemble-disagreement estimator adapted to sets. Metrics: MAE and worst-case error of predicted OOD coverage, Spearman correlation, per-shift breakdown, and calibration of the estimator's own uncertainty.
4. Closing the loop: label-free recalibration. Choose the source calibration level alpha' (via a 1-D search using the coverage estimator on unlabeled target data) so that predicted target coverage = 1-alpha; evaluate realized target coverage and mean set size. Compare against: nominal CP, importance-weighted CP, conservative alpha-inflation, and the oracle that calibrates on labeled target data. Metrics: |realized coverage - target|, fraction of shifts with under-coverage > 2 points, set-size ratio to oracle, and a size-vs-validity Pareto plot. Also report a monitoring version: a sequential test that raises an alarm when estimated coverage drops below a threshold, with ROC over shift severity.
5. Theory. Prove pseudo-coverage bias = P(B's pred not in C, Y in C) - P(B's pred in C, Y not in C); give a conditional-error-symmetry condition under which it vanishes; show that under a probit/Gaussian scaling shift model (as used to explain accuracy-on-the-line) both coverage and pseudo-coverage are probit-linear in shift intensity with matched slopes; derive a one-sided conservative bound using only model B's estimable error rate, and validate all bounds numerically against measured quantities.
6. Failure-mode map and diagnostics. Construct controlled shifts: (i) label shift by class-prior subsampling, (ii) spurious-feature shift (Waterbirds, CelebA), (iii) concept shift (relabeled classes), (iv) correlated zoos (all models fine-tuned from one backbone / same seed family). Measure where coverage-on-the-line breaks and by how much. Propose and evaluate a label-free diagnostic (pseudo-coverage line R^2 plus agreement-line slope check) that predicts whether the estimator is trustworthy; report its precision/recall for flagging cases where estimation error exceeds 5 points.
7. LLM case study. Base predictors: small open LLMs (Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B, Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B, Pythia variants) scored on multiple-choice QA. Calibrate CP on MMLU; shift to MedMCQA, ARC-Challenge, TruthfulQA-MC, and a post-cutoff/temporal set. Use cross-model pseudo-labels for pseudo-coverage. Report coverage estimation MAE and the recalibration results as in step 4, plus cost analysis (extra forward passes of a second model).

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(a) The method needs at least two (ideally several) independently trained models, doubling inference cost and possibly unavailable for proprietary settings; we will quantify how few pairs suffice. (b) Coverage-on-the-line, like agreement-on-the-line, may fail on shifts where accuracy is off the line (e.g. label shift, spurious correlations); our contribution then includes an honest failure map and diagnostics rather than universal validity. (c) Correlated zoos (shared pretraining) bias pseudo-labels toward the base model's errors, shrinking the observed bias but not the true one. (d) Guarantees are asymptotic/empirical, not finite-sample distribution-free; the label-free recalibration is a heuristic with theoretical support under stated conditions, so we will always report the conservative one-sided variant alongside. (e) Estimation noise on small target sets could dominate on WILDS subsets; we will report confidence intervals via bootstrap over pairs and points. (f) Set-size effects: on hard shifts nominal coverage may only be achievable with near-vacuous sets, which we will report explicitly.

## 4. Calibrate in FP16, Serve in INT4: Model-Shift Breaks Conformal Coverage, and Label-Free Score Coupling Fixes It

### Name

conformal_fidelity_of_compression

### Short Hypothesis

Conformal prediction is model-agnostic but not model-invariant: its guarantee requires the *same* predictor at calibration and at test time, an assumption routinely violated in deployment because the served model is quantized, pruned, distilled, recompiled, or run on a different backend than the one used for calibration. We hypothesize (i) accuracy-preserving compressions can nevertheless cause statistically significant marginal and severe class/group-conditional under-coverage, (ii) unlike data shift, this 'model shift' is *label-free identifiable* because the change in conformity score can be measured for every candidate label on unlabeled data, and (iii) a simple coupling correction using an unlabeled pool restores finite-sample validity for the served model at a small, measurable set-size cost, yielding a new 'conformal fidelity' axis on which to rank compression methods.

### Related Work

Conformal prediction theory (Vovk et al.; Angelopoulos & Bates 2021; Barber et al. 2023) treats the predictor as fixed and studies data non-exchangeability (covariate shift: Tibshirani et al. 2019; online drift: Gibbs & Candes 2021/22; label noise: Einbinder et al. 2022; Sesia et al. 2023). None of these treat a *change of the model itself between calibration and deployment*, even though weighted/adaptive fixes cannot be applied (there are no density ratios or target labels to exploit). Conformal work involving several models handles cascades/early exit (Fisch et al. 2020/21, 'Efficient conformal prediction via cascaded inference'; Schuster et al. 2022 confident adaptive language modeling) but calibrates the cascade jointly with labels, not the transfer of a calibration from one model to another; CP-for-compressed-models is, to our knowledge, unstudied. The compression literature measures accuracy and, more recently, calibration error (e.g., studies of quantization/pruning effects on ECE and OOD robustness, 'compressed models forget the tails', LLM-quantization evaluations) but never distribution-free coverage, and provides no repair mechanism with guarantees. Finally, work on inference nondeterminism (batching/backend/precision effects on LLM outputs) documents output instability but not its statistical consequences for risk control. Our contribution is (a) formalizing model shift as a new, practically dominant failure mode of CP, (b) an exactly-provable, label-free coupling correction that exploits the fact that score perturbations are observable for all candidate labels without ground truth, and (c) a compression benchmark scored by conformal fidelity rather than accuracy.

### Abstract

Conformal prediction is celebrated as model-agnostic, and this is often misread as model-invariant: in practice calibration is performed once, with an expensive full-precision checkpoint, while the model actually serving requests is quantized, pruned, distilled, compiled, or executed on a different backend or batch size. We show that this seemingly innocuous mismatch is a distinct source of guarantee failure, model shift, which existing remedies for distribution shift cannot address because there is neither a covariate likelihood ratio nor target labels to adapt to. Empirically, INT4/INT8 post-training quantization and moderate pruning of ImageNet classifiers and open LLMs leave top-1 accuracy nearly intact yet perturb conformity scores enough to push coverage of APS/RAPS/THR sets below nominal, with far larger deficits for rare classes and hard subgroups; reverse mismatch wastes efficiency instead. Our key observation is that, unlike data shift, model shift is identifiable without labels: for any unlabeled input, the score change can be computed for every candidate label, so the worst-case per-example perturbation is an observable quantity. We turn this into Coupled Conformal Calibration: using a labeled calibration set scored by the old model plus an unlabeled pool scored by both models, an order-statistic bound on the perturbation quantile yields prediction sets for the served model with finite-sample coverage at least 1-alpha-beta, together with a tighter (heuristic) monotone score-transport variant. We evaluate coverage, set size, label budget, and conditional validity across compression families, and propose 'conformal fidelity', the alpha-inflation a compression requires, as a new deployment metric.

### Experiments

1. Theory. Let scores be smaller-is-better, sets C(x)={y: s(x,y)<=q}. With calibration model A and served model B define the observable per-example coupling gap Delta(x)=max_y [s_B(x,y)-s_A(x,y)], computable from unlabeled x. Prove C_A^q(x) subseteq C_B^{q+Delta(x)}(x), hence using a global delta = the (1-beta) empirical quantile of Delta over an unlabeled pool of size m gives P(Y in C_B^{q+delta}(X)) >= 1-alpha-beta, with an exact finite-sample statement via order statistics (and a variant with (alpha,beta) budget optimization to minimize expected set size). Also derive (a) the exact coverage loss of naive transfer as a function of the score-perturbation distribution near the calibration quantile, showing it is governed by P(s_A<=q<s_B), a quantity estimable without labels only under a stated exchangeability-of-labels condition, and (b) a two-sided version detecting over-coverage/efficiency loss. Validate all formulas by Monte Carlo on measured score distributions.
2. How bad is model shift? Vision: ~15 timm ImageNet models (ResNet-50, ViT-S/B, ConvNeXt, MobileNet) x compressions {fp16, bf16, INT8 PTQ, INT4 (GPTQ-style / bitsandbytes), 20/50/80% magnitude and structured pruning, knowledge-distilled student, torch.compile, CPU-vs-GPU, TF32 on/off, batch size 1 vs 128}. LLM: Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B, Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B on MMLU/ARC/MedMCQA multiple choice with AWQ/GPTQ/NF4 weights, KV-cache quantization, and HF-vs-vLLM backends. Calibrate split CP (THR/APS/RAPS, alpha in {0.05,0.1,0.2}) on the uncompressed model, evaluate coverage of the served compressed model on held-out labeled test data. Metrics: marginal coverage gap with binomial CIs, fraction of (model, compression, alpha) cells with significant under-coverage, worst-class and worst-group (rare classes, ImageNet-C severities, subject-wise for MMLU) coverage, mean set size change, and score-flip rate at the quantile. Report accuracy change alongside to show accuracy-preserving != coverage-preserving.
3. Nondeterminism stress test. Same checkpoint, different serving conditions (batch size, backend, attention kernel, speculative decoding for generation, dtype of the softmax) and repeated runs; test whether coverage deviations are statistically detectable at n = 1k/10k, and quantify the minimum calibration size at which each source becomes material. Include one generative task (conformal factuality-style filtering of long-form answers) to check whether decoding-level nondeterminism perturbs risk control.
4. Fixes and their cost. Compare: (i) naive transfer (no correction); (ii) conservative alpha inflation by a fixed margin; (iii) our Coupled Conformal Calibration with unlabeled pool sizes m in {200, 1k, 10k} and beta in {0.01, 0.02, 0.05}; (iv) monotone score transport: fit an isotonic map from s_A to s_B on the unlabeled paired scores, push the calibration scores through it, then take the quantile; (v) gold standard: recalibrate with fresh labels on the served model (varying label budget). Metrics: realized coverage on the served model, mean/median set size, size relative to gold standard, and label budget required; report Pareto curves of validity vs efficiency vs labeling cost.
5. Conformal fidelity leaderboard. Define CF(compression) = the smallest alpha-inflation (or the delta from step 1) needed to keep served coverage >= 1-alpha across datasets, and rank compression methods/bit-widths by CF. Test whether CF correlates with standard proxies (accuracy drop, ECE change, KL between output distributions, weight-error norm) - if CF is poorly predicted by accuracy/ECE, it is a genuinely new axis.
6. Heterogeneous serving. Simulate load-dependent routing between fp16 and INT4 replicas with unknown, time-varying mixture weights; show naive CP under-covers and prove/evaluate that the coupling correction computed against the worst replica gives a valid guarantee for any mixture, comparing size cost to per-replica calibration.

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(a) The coverage deficit from mild compressions may be small at practical calibration sizes; the paper's value then shifts to mapping exactly which compressions (INT4, aggressive pruning, distillation, rare classes) are unsafe, plus the guarantee-preserving correction. (b) The coupling bound uses a worst-case-over-labels gap, so it can be conservative for large label spaces (mitigated by restricting the max to a superset of plausible labels, e.g. top-k of the served model, at the cost of an extra small assumption we will state and empirically test). (c) The correction requires an unlabeled pool that can be scored by the expensive model offline; environments where the original model is discarded cannot use it. (d) Score transport assumes a roughly monotone, label-independent relation between old and new scores; we test this but it is not guaranteed. (e) Generative-task experiments have noisier metrics and may need automatic factuality judging, adding evaluation noise. (f) We use small/mid-size open models and post-training compression only; conclusions about frontier-scale quantization are extrapolations.

## 5. How Many Labels Does a Conformal Guarantee Cost? Active, Label-Efficient Calibration with Finite-Sample Validity

### Name

active_calibration_label_efficient_cp

### Short Hypothesis

Split conformal prediction spends hundreds to thousands of expensive ground-truth labels to estimate a single scalar quantile, and most of those labels are uninformative because the model is obviously right (or obviously wrong) on them. We hypothesize that (i) which calibration points get labeled matters enormously, (ii) by combining a model-derived proxy for the coverage indicator as a control variate with variance-optimal, adaptively chosen labeling probabilities and Horvitz-Thompson debiasing, one can certify 1-alpha coverage with 2-10x fewer labels than random-sample split conformal at the same set size, and (iii) this can be done with exact finite-sample (PAC / training-conditional) validity despite the labeling design being adaptive and non-exchangeable. We further hypothesize a matching lower bound: the achievable saving is governed by the 'ambiguity mass' of the score distribution near the calibration threshold, so label-efficient calibration is provably impossible exactly when the model's confidence is uninformative.

### Related Work

Split conformal prediction (Vovk et al.; Lei et al.; Angelopoulos & Bates 2021) assumes a labeled calibration set drawn exchangeably with the test point and gives marginal coverage; rules of thumb (n >= ~1000 for alpha=0.1) are widely quoted, and the sample-size question has been studied only for i.i.d. random calibration data (Vovk 2012 and Bian & Barber 2023 for training-conditional/PAC bounds; Park et al. 2020 PAC prediction sets). None of this literature asks whether the calibration labels should be *chosen*. Active learning literature uses conformal or uncertainty scores to select points for *training*, not for calibration, and offers no coverage guarantee for the resulting predictor; conversely, semi-supervised/transductive conformal work uses unlabeled data to build better scores, not to reduce calibration labeling cost. Prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al. 2023) and cross-PPI use a proxy model plus a small labeled set to estimate means/quantiles, and are the closest prior tool; but PPI assumes a fixed (typically uniform i.i.d.) labeled subsample, targets a population parameter rather than a validity-certified prediction set, and does not exploit the fact that in conformal calibration the *entire* candidate-score profile s(x,y) for all y is computable on unlabeled data, which makes an unusually strong control variate available and makes optimal allocation explicit. Robust/weighted conformal methods (Tibshirani et al. 2019; Barber et al. 2023) reweight for distribution shift with unknown weights, whereas our sampling weights are known by design, allowing exact finite-sample guarantees via Horvitz-Thompson estimation and betting supermartingales (Waudby-Smith & Ramdas 2020). To our knowledge, active label acquisition for conformal calibration with finite-sample valid coverage, together with a label-complexity lower bound, is unaddressed.

### Abstract

Conformal prediction is usually presented as free: attach it to any model and get 1-alpha coverage. It is not free -- it costs ground-truth labels, the scarcest resource in exactly the high-stakes domains (pathology, radiology, expert QA) where prediction sets are wanted. We revisit calibration as a label-acquisition problem and observe that split conformal prediction is statistically wasteful: it burns labels to learn one scalar threshold, and the vast majority of labeled points contribute an essentially deterministic coverage indicator because the model is confidently right. We propose ACTIVE-CAL, which (1) computes, for every unlabeled pool point, the model-implied probability that the point would be covered at a candidate threshold -- a control variate available because all candidate-label scores are computable without labels; (2) allocates label queries with probabilities proportional to the residual variance of the coverage indicator, i.e. to genuinely ambiguous points; (3) forms a Horvitz-Thompson debiased coverage estimate whose bias is zero regardless of proxy quality; and (4) selects the smallest threshold whose anytime-valid betting lower confidence bound on coverage exceeds 1-alpha, yielding prediction sets with exact finite-sample PAC (training-conditional) coverage under an adaptive, non-exchangeable design. We prove a label-complexity upper bound scaling with the ambiguity mass near the threshold and a matching lower bound showing no method can do better when model confidence is uninformative. Across ImageNet/CIFAR-100 classifiers, medical imaging, LLM multiple-choice QA, and CQR regression, ACTIVE-CAL matches the coverage and set size of random split conformal with 2-10x fewer labels, and we characterize when it does not.

### Experiments

1. Theory. (a) Fix a nested family of sets C_t(x) = {y : s(x,y) <= t}, define g(t) = P(Y in C_t(X)) and the proxy g_hat(t) = E_x[sum_y p_hat(y|x) 1{s(x,y)<=t}]. Show the Horvitz-Thompson + control-variate estimator of g(t) under labeling probabilities pi(x) is unbiased for any pi > 0 and any proxy, derive the variance-minimizing pi*(x) proportional to sqrt(v(x)) with v(x) the proxy residual variance, and quantify the variance ratio to uniform sampling as a function of the distribution of p_hat. (b) Build an anytime-valid lower confidence bound on g(t) simultaneously over t (via a weighted empirical-Bernstein / betting supermartingale plus an importance-weighted DKW-type band), so the same labels certify all alpha simultaneously; output t_hat = min{t : LCB(g(t)) >= 1-alpha} and prove P(coverage(t_hat) >= 1-alpha) >= 1-delta. (c) Prove a lower bound: for any adaptive querying strategy, the number of labels needed to certify coverage within epsilon is Omega(A/epsilon^2 * log(1/delta)) where A is the ambiguity mass (variance of the coverage indicator conditional on the model's proxy) near the threshold, so gains vanish for uninformative proxies. Validate all bounds by Monte Carlo on measured score distributions.
2. Core benchmark (classification). Pretrained models from timm on ImageNet-val and ResNet/ViT on CIFAR-100; scores THR, APS, RAPS; alpha in {0.05, 0.1, 0.2}; delta = 0.1. Simulate labeling by revealing held-out labels only for queried points. Label budgets B in {50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000}. Compare: (i) random split conformal (marginal quantile), (ii) random split conformal with PAC/training-conditional correction (Clopper-Pearson / Vovk 2012 bound), (iii) stratified-by-confidence sampling, (iv) PPI/PPI++ quantile estimation with a uniform subsample, (v) ACTIVE-CAL (control variate only), (vi) ACTIVE-CAL (control variate + optimal allocation), (vii) oracle (full labeled pool). Metrics over 500 random repetitions: distribution of realized test coverage (mean, 5th percentile, fraction of runs under-covering), mean set size, and 'labels to reach (1-alpha, delta)-validity at oracle set size'. Report label-savings factor per method/score/alpha.
3. Where it breaks. Ablate proxy quality: temperature-scaled vs raw vs deliberately miscalibrated (label-smoothed, overconfident) models; heavy distribution shift between pool and test (ImageNet-C/R, CIFAR-10.1); flat score distributions from a weak/random model. Check that validity is preserved (it must be, by construction) and measure how savings degrade toward 1x, confirming the lower-bound story.
4. Cost-aware and high-stakes settings. (a) Heterogeneous label costs (cheap crowd label vs expert label) with a budget constraint: extend allocation to pi proportional to sqrt(v(x)/cost(x)) and evaluate cost-to-validity. (b) Medical imaging with real expensive labels: Camelyon17/PathMNIST/CheXpert-derived multi-label or ISIC skin lesion classification; report labels/dollars saved. (c) Class/group-conditional (Mondrian) calibration, where per-group label budgets bind hardest: measure worst-group coverage vs budget against uniform per-group sampling.
5. Beyond classification. (a) Regression with CQR on UCI/medical-expenditure data: the coverage indicator proxy comes from the model's predictive distribution; report interval length vs label budget. (b) LLM multiple-choice QA (MMLU, MedMCQA) with Qwen2.5/Llama-3 logit scores where 'labels' represent expensive expert verification. (c) Conformal risk control style monotone loss (e.g., false-negative rate for multi-label): show the same machinery certifies risk <= alpha with fewer labels.
6. Practical recipe and multi-alpha reuse. Show that one adaptively labeled calibration set, via the simultaneous-in-t band, supports many alpha values and several score functions at once, and quantify the price of simultaneity versus per-alpha calibration.

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(a) The magnitude of savings depends entirely on proxy informativeness; for weak or badly miscalibrated models savings may be near 1x, in which case the paper's contribution becomes the framework plus the sharp characterization (and lower bound) of when label-efficient calibration is possible. (b) PAC/training-conditional guarantees are strictly stronger than marginal coverage and therefore inherently costlier; comparisons must be made at matched guarantee type, and marginal-coverage split conformal will look cheaper unless we also report its high variance in realized coverage at small n (we will report both). (c) Horvitz-Thompson weights inflate variance when pi(x) is small; we will floor pi and report sensitivity. (d) Optimal allocation depends on the unknown threshold t, requiring a two-stage or sequential scheme whose finite-sample validity relies on the betting/supermartingale argument -- correctness of the adaptive analysis is the main technical risk. (e) Simulated labeling reveals held-out labels and thus ignores annotation noise and cost heterogeneity in real workflows; the medical experiments only approximate this. (f) Prediction-powered inference is a strong baseline and may capture much of the gain from the control variate alone, so we must clearly isolate the additional benefit of adaptive allocation and of the simultaneous-in-threshold certification.

## 6. Your Prediction Set Is Someone Else's Data: Conformal Calibration as a Privacy Side Channel

### Name

conformal_privacy_side_channel

### Short Hypothesis

Split conformal prediction publishes, through every prediction set it emits, a threshold that is literally the conformity score of one individual calibration record (an order statistic), and in online/adaptive conformal prediction that threshold moves with other users' realized outcomes. We hypothesize that (i) an adversary with only black-box set-valued query access can extract the calibration quantile to near-machine precision, (ii) this yields membership inference against the calibration set that is substantially stronger than standard training-data MIA against the same model, especially for small calibration sets, and near-certain identification of the 'quantile individual', (iii) in adaptive conformal prediction the threshold trajectory leaks other users' labels/correctness, and (iv) rank-randomized and DP quantile defenses can suppress these attacks at a quantifiable, and surprisingly small, set-size cost.

### Related Work

Conformal prediction theory (Vovk et al.; Lei et al.; Angelopoulos & Bates 2021) treats the calibration set as a statistical resource and never as sensitive data; the calibration quantile is an order statistic, hence an individual record, but this is only ever discussed as a source of variance (Vovk 2012; Bian & Barber 2023 training-conditional coverage), never as leakage. Private Prediction Sets (Angelopoulos et al. 2021) and follow-ups on DP/federated conformal prediction (e.g., federated CP under label shift; DP quantile estimation via the exponential mechanism, Smith et al./Gillenwater et al.) supply defenses but assume, rather than demonstrate, a threat: they present no attack, no threat model over set-valued query access, and no empirical privacy-efficiency Pareto that a practitioner can use to choose epsilon. The membership-inference literature (Shokri et al. 2017; Carlini et al. 2022 LiRA; Ye et al. 2022) exclusively targets training data and uses confidence/loss signals; nobody has attacked the calibration stage, which is a distinct and arguably softer target because n is small (hundreds to thousands) and the released statistic is an individual score. Adaptive/online conformal prediction (Gibbs & Candes 2021, 2022; conformal PID) updates the threshold from streaming labels and is deployed in shared-service settings; the resulting cross-user information flow (an attacker inferring other users' outcomes from threshold drift) has, to our knowledge, never been analyzed. Our contribution is thus the missing threat model plus concrete attacks (threshold extraction, calibration-set MIA, online label leakage), a matching theory of attack advantage and DP sensitivity in rank space, and the first measured privacy/coverage/efficiency trade-off for conformal deployment.

### Abstract

Conformal prediction is sold as a costless wrapper that turns any predictor into one with distribution-free coverage. We show it also turns any predictor into a leak. The wrapper's only learned parameter is a threshold equal to the ceil((n+1)(1-alpha))-th smallest conformity score of the calibration set: not an aggregate, but a single individual's score. We formalize a black-box threat model in which an adversary observes only prediction sets, and show that (a) the threshold can be recovered to arbitrary precision with O(log(1/eps)) queries by binary search along a continuous path in input space (e.g., interpolating between two images or perturbing a prompt), because set membership flips exactly when a score crosses the threshold; (b) once the threshold is known, testing whether a candidate record was in the calibration set reduces to comparing its score to the threshold, giving near-certain identification of the quantile record and, via a shadow-calibration likelihood-ratio test, materially elevated membership advantage for the rest of the calibration set; and (c) in adaptive conformal prediction the threshold is updated by other users' outcomes, so its trajectory leaks whether other users' predictions were covered, a cross-user side channel we exploit to reconstruct hidden labels. We prove that the empirical-quantile release has maximal individual sensitivity in rank space and derive attack-advantage bounds matching our measurements on CIFAR-100, ImageNet, Camelyon17 pathology, and LLM multiple-choice QA. Finally, we evaluate defenses -- rank randomization, subsample-and-aggregate thresholds, exponential-mechanism DP quantiles, and rate-limited/batched online updates -- and chart the resulting privacy-coverage-efficiency Pareto, finding that near-complete attack suppression often costs only a few percent in set size.

### Experiments

1. Theory. (a) Show that for continuous scores the released statistic q_hat equals s(X_(k), Y_(k)) for the k-th order statistic, so releasing q_hat exactly reveals one calibration record's score; formalize threshold extraction: if the adversary can generate inputs whose score varies continuously and monotonically along a path (verified empirically via interpolation), set-membership flips localize q_hat to precision eps in ceil(log2(range/eps)) queries. (b) Derive the Bayes-optimal membership test given q_hat: for a candidate (x,y), the likelihood ratio of 'in calibration' vs 'out' under a shadow score distribution, and an upper bound on advantage as a function of n, alpha, and the local density of scores at q_hat; show advantage for a random calibration member scales like ~1/n while identification of the quantile member is near-perfect. (c) Rank-space sensitivity: replacing one calibration point can shift q_hat by the gap between adjacent order statistics, which is unbounded in score space; hence any pure-DP release requires rank-space mechanisms (exponential mechanism) and we derive the resulting coverage/size inflation as a function of (eps, n, alpha).
2. Threshold extraction in practice. Vision: timm ImageNet models and ResNet/ViT on CIFAR-100 with THR/APS/RAPS scores; construct query paths by convex interpolation between two images, by increasing Gaussian noise, and by adversarially scaling logits-inducing perturbations; measure recovery error of q_hat vs number of queries, and whether monotonicity failures require a bisection-with-restarts variant. LLM: Qwen2.5-7B/Llama-3-8B on MMLU with token-logit scores, using paraphrase interpolation and distractor edits as the query path. Metric: |q_hat_est - q_hat| and queries needed for 1e-3 precision; also test extraction when the API returns only top-k sets or randomized tie-breaking.
3. Calibration-set membership inference. Given extracted q_hat, run: (i) exact-match attack identifying the quantile record from a candidate pool of size m in {100, 1000, 10000} (metric: top-1 identification rate vs pool size and score-collision rate); (ii) LiRA-style shadow-calibration attack for generic membership: train shadow calibration sets, model the distribution of q_hat with/without a target record, and score candidates by likelihood ratio (metrics: AUC, TPR at 0.1%/1% FPR); (iii) multi-query amplification, where the adversary observes q_hat under multiple alpha values or several recalibration rounds (each reveals a different order statistic), showing advantage grows with the number of released levels. Compare directly against standard training-data MIA (loss-based and LiRA) on the same model, sweeping calibration size n in {100, 500, 1000, 5000}: headline test of whether calibration leakage exceeds training leakage.
4. Sensitive-domain case study. Camelyon17/PathMNIST or ISIC skin-lesion classification, where calibration data are patient records: report identification rate and TPR@1%FPR, plus an attribute-inference variant (infer the label of an identified calibration record from its position relative to q_hat).
5. Online conformal label side channel. Deploy adaptive conformal inference (Gibbs & Candes) in a shared multi-tenant setting: threshold q_t updates by +gamma*alpha or -gamma*(1-alpha) after each other-user outcome. An attacker issues one probe query per round to observe q_t via extraction, and decodes the sign of each update. Metrics: per-round label-reconstruction accuracy of other users' coverage indicators, downstream reconstruction of their labels when the input is guessable/known, and how accuracy degrades with batched updates, added noise, probe-rate limits, and multiple concurrent users.
6. Defenses and Pareto. Evaluate (i) rank randomization: use order statistic k + Z with Z discrete-Laplace/uniform, with a corrected coverage guarantee; (ii) subsample-and-aggregate: median of quantiles from disjoint calibration shards; (iii) exponential-mechanism DP quantile (Angelopoulos et al. private prediction sets) across eps in {0.5, 1, 2, 8}; (iv) score discretization/coarse set reporting to block extraction; (v) for online: mini-batch updates and DP-SGD-style noise on the threshold. For each, report empirical coverage (must remain >= 1-alpha or its DP-corrected variant), mean set size relative to undefended CP, and all attack metrics from steps 2-5, producing a privacy-coverage-efficiency Pareto plot and a practical recommendation table indexed by n and alpha.
7. Audit tool. Package the attacks as a lightweight audit that, given query access to a conformal service, returns an empirical lower bound on the eps it satisfies (via the standard MIA-to-eps conversion), and run it on our own defended deployments to check tightness.

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(a) Score paths may be non-monotone or non-smooth, making threshold extraction noisier than the idealized bisection; we address this with restarts and report failure rates, and extraction may be infeasible for APIs that return heavily quantized or randomized sets. (b) For generic calibration members, the information-theoretic advantage from a single scalar order statistic is inherently O(1/n), so aggregate MIA AUCs may be modest; the striking result is the exact identification of the quantile record and the multi-level amplification, and we must present the modest-advantage regime honestly. (c) DP conformal prediction already exists as a defense, so the paper's contribution is threat modeling, attacks, and the empirical trade-off rather than a wholly new mechanism; there is a risk reviewers see the vulnerability as folklore, which we mitigate by demonstrating the attacks end-to-end and by the previously unexamined online cross-user channel. (d) The online attack assumes the adversary can probe every round and that updates are per-example; batched or delayed deployments will attenuate it (we quantify this). (e) Real-world calibration sets may be public benchmarks, in which case privacy is moot; our sensitive-domain case study is a proxy for genuinely private calibration pipelines (hospital recalibration, federated CP). (f) Shadow-calibration attacks require knowing the score function and an approximate data distribution; we ablate mismatch in both.
