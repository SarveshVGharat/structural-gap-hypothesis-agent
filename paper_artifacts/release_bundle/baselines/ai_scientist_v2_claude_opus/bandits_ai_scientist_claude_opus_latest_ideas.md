## 1. Exploration Is a Broadcast Channel: Property Inference from Bandit Action Logs and the Regret-Secrecy Frontier

### Name

action_log_leakage_bandits

### Short Hypothesis

The sequence of arms pulled by a bandit algorithm -- with no reward information whatsoever -- is nearly a sufficient statistic for the reward means the algorithm has learned, so any low-regret algorithm necessarily broadcasts its private conclusions to any passive observer; there is a quantifiable Pareto frontier between regret and 'aggregate secrecy' that differential privacy for bandits does not address at all.

### Related Work

Three lines are adjacent but distinct. (1) Differential privacy in bandits (Mishra & Thakurta; Shariff & Sheffet; joint-DP contextual bandits; recent 'Faster Rates for Private Adversarial Bandits') protects an *individual* reward observation from an adversary who sees the action stream; by design it still permits -- indeed guarantees -- learning and hence revealing the *population-level* conclusion (which arm is best, what the gaps are). We study exactly this orthogonal, aggregate leakage, analogous to property/distribution-inference attacks in supervised learning (Ateniese et al.; Suri & Evans) which have never been formulated for sequential decision making. (2) Inverse/structural estimation of decision makers: 'Learning from a Learner' (Jacq et al., 2019) and inverse RL recover rewards from a learning agent's policies, and econometric dynamic-discrete-choice work estimates payoffs from choices; these aim to build an imitator and assume rich access (policies, states, sometimes rewards). We instead treat the action log as a side channel, give algorithm-aware maximum-likelihood attacks that exploit the *deterministic index structure* of UCB/elimination (switch times encode empirical means), and prove attacker-side sample-complexity guarantees plus algorithm-side impossibility results. (3) Adversarial attacks on bandits (reward poisoning, attacks on cooperative multi-agent bandits) and free-riding/communication in multi-agent bandits assume reward or message channels; our observer is strictly weaker (actions only, no rewards, no communication), which makes both the attack and the free-riding result surprising. To our knowledge no prior work defines an information-theoretic secrecy notion for the *identity of the optimal arm*, nor characterizes its price in regret.

### Abstract

Bandit algorithms are usually analyzed as if their action stream were a private byproduct of learning. In practice the action stream is the most public thing about a deployed system: displayed prices, shown ads, recommended items, and prescribed treatments are all observable, while rewards are not. We ask a simple question: how much does a bandit algorithm reveal to an observer who sees only which arm was pulled at each round? We formalize a passive eavesdropper performing property inference on the action log, with two targets: identifying the optimal arm and estimating the gap vector. First, we show that action logs are startlingly informative. Beyond a naive argmax-of-counts attack, we derive algorithm-aware likelihood attacks that invert the deterministic index dynamics of UCB and successive elimination: the times at which the played arm switches pin down the empirical means, letting an observer estimate gaps at a rate within a constant factor of an observer who saw all the rewards. A sequential Monte Carlo attack extends this to Thompson Sampling and EXP3. Second, we prove this leakage is unavoidable: via a Fano/change-of-measure argument, any algorithm with regret R_T admits an attacker whose best-arm error probability is O(R_T / (Delta T)), so epsilon-secrecy forces linear regret with an explicit constant, yielding a matching regret-secrecy frontier. We then design decoy-set and count-symmetrization wrappers that attain this frontier, and show that DP-bandit algorithms sit far from it. Finally we flip the attack into an algorithm: a second agent that merely watches a UCB agent achieves near-optimal regret with zero exploration cost, making exploration a public good. Experiments on synthetic bandits and real recommendation logs quantify per-algorithm leakage.

### Experiments

1) Attack implementation and evaluation (synthetic K-armed Bernoulli/Gaussian bandits, K in {5,10,50}, T up to 1e5, gap profiles from easy to hard). Observers see only a_1..a_T. Attacks: (a) Count-argmax; (b) Switch-time MLE for UCB1/KL-UCB/Successive Elimination: given the known algorithm and observed actions, solve for the set of empirical-mean trajectories consistent with 'played arm had the largest index at every round', via a constrained least-squares / interval-propagation estimator on index crossings, outputting a gap estimate; (c) Sequential importance sampling ('particle inversion') for Thompson Sampling and EXP3, where each particle carries a hypothesized reward history reweighted by the probability the algorithm would have chosen the observed action. Metrics vs. round t: best-arm identification accuracy, L2 error of gap estimates, and the *leakage ratio* = attacker error divided by the error of an oracle observer who sees all rewards; also empirical mutual information I(argmax arm ; a_1..t) estimated by Monte Carlo over random instances. Prediction to test: leakage ratio is O(1) for deterministic index algorithms and grows for randomized ones.
2) Leakage audit across algorithms: UCB1, KL-UCB, Successive Elimination, ETC, Thompson Sampling, EXP3, epsilon-greedy, and DP variants (DP-SE, private UCB at eps in {0.1,1,10}). Report 'leakage time' tau_0.9 = first round the best attack reaches 90% best-arm accuracy, alongside regret. Key comparison: DP algorithms should not increase tau_0.9 much, empirically demonstrating that DP does not buy aggregate secrecy.
3) Lower bound validation: instantiate the theoretical bound epsilon-secrecy => regret >= c*eps*Delta*T on 2- and 3-armed instances by grid-searching over parameterized algorithms (mixtures of committed policies) and plotting achieved (regret, attacker error) pairs against the theoretical frontier.
4) Frontier algorithms: implement (i) Decoy-m: run successive elimination but stop eliminating once m candidates remain and thereafter play the survivors with symmetrized counts in a randomly permuted round-robin; (ii) Count-symmetrization wrapper: given any base algorithm, play its recommendation but maintain a randomized 'shadow budget' that forces the play-count vector of the top-m arms to be exchangeable; (iii) Delayed-commit: postpone commitment past a leakage budget. Measure regret vs. best-achievable attacker accuracy, and verify the predicted Pareto shape; compare against the lower bound.
5) Real data: Open Bandit Dataset and MovieLens-1M converted to a K-armed / linear bandit via replay. Run UCB and TS, run the attacks on the action logs, and report attacker accuracy in identifying the top item and in ranking items (Kendall tau vs. true means). Linear-bandit extension: attacker estimates theta direction from the action log of LinUCB; metric = cosine similarity to true theta over time.
6) The dual 'free-riding' experiment: agent B observes only agent A's actions (A runs UCB, B pulls arms in its own copy of the environment and gets rewards but no exploration budget beyond following B's inferred belief). Compare B's regret to (a) running its own UCB, (b) an oracle told the best arm. Also test the adversarial converse: does running a frontier algorithm from (4) measurably slow the free-rider, and at what cost to the leader's own regret?

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(1) The headline impossibility result may be viewed as intuitive: exploiting the best arm necessarily reveals it. We mitigate this by making the statement quantitative (an explicit constant-level frontier with matching upper and lower bounds) and by focusing the surprise on the *gap-estimation* attack, where the claim that action-only observation matches reward-level observation up to constants is non-obvious. (2) Algorithm-aware inversion of randomized algorithms (TS, EXP3) may be statistically or computationally hard; particle degeneracy could weaken those results, in which case the finding 'randomization is a cheap partial defense' becomes the contribution. (3) The MLE/interval-propagation attack assumes knowledge of the algorithm and its parameters; we will report degradation under algorithm mismatch, but a fully algorithm-agnostic attack may be much weaker. (4) Formal secrecy definitions (mutual information vs. attacker error probability) may not be interchangeable; we will report both and prove relations only where clean. (5) Real-data replay evaluation of bandits is noisy and off-policy; conclusions there are indicative rather than definitive. (6) Non-stationary, contextual, or delayed-feedback settings may substantially change the leakage picture and are left partly to future work.

## 2. One Bit Per Pull: The Bit Complexity of Bandit Exploration and the Optimal Granularity of Feedback

### Name

bit_complexity_bandits

### Short Hypothesis

Sample complexity is the wrong currency when observations are expensive to *measure* rather than to *obtain*: we hypothesize that bandit learning has a distinct bit complexity, that a single adaptively placed bit per pull is enough to match full-precision instance-dependent regret up to constants for sub-Gaussian rewards, and that non-interactive (fixed-resolution) feedback provably needs Theta(log(1/sigma)) bits per pull, yielding a concrete design rule -- feedback resolution should match observation noise, not the reward gap.

### Related Work

(1) Quantization for communication in distributed/federated bandits: 'Solving Multi-Arm Bandit Using a Few Bits of Communication' (Hanna, Yang & Fragouli, AISTATS 2022) and communication-efficient federated bandit papers show that a small fixed number of bits per reward preserves near-optimal *minimax* sqrt(KT) regret. These works fix a quantizer designed by a system engineer to cut network cost; they do not treat resolution as an adaptively controlled decision variable, do not characterize instance-dependent (variance-dependent) bit complexity, and give no lower bounds separating interactive from non-interactive quantization. (2) Variance-adaptive bandits (UCB-V, Bernstein/KL-UCB) and heavy-tailed bandits (Bubeck, Cesa-Bianchi & Lugosi) assume full-precision rewards; we ask what part of their variance adaptivity survives coarse feedback, and show quantization is essentially an implicit robust mean estimator, so coarse feedback can be *better* than fine feedback under heavy tails. (3) One-bit/quantized estimation and interactive vs non-interactive local privacy (Duchi et al.; one-bit compressed sensing; signSGD/QSGD) study estimation or optimization with coarse observations, but never the exploration-measurement coupling: in a bandit, placing a good threshold requires knowing the mean you are trying to estimate, and the arm you must measure precisely changes over time. (4) Dueling/preference bandits and thresholding bandits involve one-bit feedback but with an externally fixed comparison structure; we let the learner *design its own query* (which arm, which threshold, how many bits) under an explicit bit budget. (5) Bandits with censored/binary feedback in operations research assume a fixed censoring level. Our contribution -- a bit-complexity theory with matching upper/lower bounds, an interactivity separation, and an empirical rating-scale design rule for human feedback -- is orthogonal to all of these.

### Abstract

Bandit theory measures the cost of learning in pulls, implicitly assuming each pull reveals a real number for free. In many deployments the expensive resource is not the pull but the *resolution* of the observation: a human annotator can give a thumbs-up cheaply or a fine-grained score expensively, a sensor can report a coarse bin or a calibrated value, and a client can transmit one bit or a float. We introduce the bit-budgeted bandit, in which the learner selects an arm and a quantizer (a set of at most m bits' worth of measurement questions about the realized reward) each round, and is charged for bits rather than rounds. We develop the resulting bit complexity theory. First, we show that classical variance adaptivity is fragile under blind quantization: with any fixed, history-independent quantizer of m bits, the achievable instance-dependent regret scales with max(sigma^2, 4^{-m}) log T / Delta, so attaining full-precision performance requires Theta(log(1/sigma)) bits per pull -- resolution must be matched to the noise level, not to the gap. Second, we show interactivity removes this cost: an adaptive-window dithering scheme (AQ-UCB) that shrinks its measurement range with its own confidence interval attains sigma^2 log T / Delta regret with a single bit per pull for sub-Gaussian rewards, giving a provable interactive/non-interactive separation. Third, we show quantization is an implicit robust mean estimator, so one-bit feedback matches truncated-mean bandits under heavy tails without moment tuning. Simulations validate the predicted scalings, and replay experiments on recommendation data instantiate the theory as a concrete design rule for how many levels a feedback scale should have.

### Experiments

1) Predicted-scaling validation (synthetic). K-armed Gaussian and Beta bandits with noise sigma in {0.5, 0.2, 0.05, 0.01} and gaps Delta in {0.3, 0.1, 0.03}, T up to 2e5. Algorithms: (a) full-precision UCB-V/KL-UCB (upper baseline); (b) fixed uniform m-bit quantizer + UCB for m in {1,2,4,8}; (c) 1-bit global dithering (observe 1{X_t > U_t}, U_t ~ Unif[0,1], an unbiased mean oracle) + UCB; (d) proposed AQ-UCB: maintain per-arm mean estimate and confidence width w_a, dither only inside the clipped window [mu_hat_a - c*w_a - c'*sigma_hat_a, mu_hat_a + c*w_a + c'*sigma_hat_a] and de-bias the clipped estimate; (e) shape-aware 1-bit: threshold at the running mean estimate and invert the Gaussian CDF. Metrics: cumulative regret vs t; empirical estimator variance per bit; fitted exponent of regret vs sigma. Prediction to falsify: (b)/(c) plateau at a sigma-independent regret floor while (d)/(e) track (a) within a constant factor.
2) Bit-budget frontier and allocation. Impose a total budget B bits (rather than T rounds) and let each algorithm choose rounds/resolution freely; plot cumulative and simple regret vs B. Include an explicit 'resolution allocator' that solves min_m m*max(sigma_hat_a^2, 4^{-m}) per arm online, testing the rule m* ~ log_4(1/sigma_hat_a^2). Report bits-to-identify-best-arm at 95% confidence and compare to predicted Theta(sigma^2 log(1/sigma)/Delta^2) non-interactive vs Theta(sigma^2/Delta^2) interactive.
3) Interactivity separation, empirically tight. On 2- and 3-armed instances, grid-search over parameterized 1-bit strategies restricted to (i) history-independent quantizers and (ii) history-adaptive quantizers; plot achieved regret against the theoretical lower bound curve to confirm the gap is real and matches constants.
4) Adversarial-to-clipping and heavy-tailed distributions. Bimodal rewards, Bernoulli with rare large payoff, Pareto(alpha=1.5) and lognormal arms. Compare AQ-UCB, 1-bit dithering with a learned dynamic range, truncated-mean UCB and median-of-means UCB. Metric: regret and failure rate; test the claim that coarse feedback matches or beats full precision under heavy tails (quantization as free robustness), and that clipping bias, not variance, is the binding constraint for bimodal rewards.
5) Real-data granularity design. MovieLens-25M and the Open Bandit Dataset via replay. Simulate annotation with L-level scales (L in {2,3,5,10,continuous}), charging log2(L) bits per label under a fixed total bit budget, with annotator noise injected at levels estimated from within-user rating dispersion. Question answered: at a fixed human-annotation bit budget, are many binary judgments better than fewer fine-grained ratings? Check whether the empirically best L matches the theory's noise-matched prediction L* ~ 1/sigma, and whether interactive granularity (coarse early, fine near the top arms) beats any fixed L.
6) Small tie-in: because 1-bit dithered feedback is exactly randomized response, report the locally-private regret obtained 'for free' by AQ-UCB and compare with LDP bandit baselines at matched epsilon.

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(1) The unbiased-one-bit trick (dithering) and adaptive one-bit mean estimation are known in estimation and local-privacy literatures; novelty rests on the bandit-specific coupling, the instance-dependent bit-complexity characterization, and the interactivity separation -- if a prior bandit paper already establishes the variance-matched adaptive result, the contribution narrows to lower bounds and the granularity design rule. (2) The non-interactive lower bound must quantify over all m-bit quantizers; a fully general argument may only be provable for restricted classes (e.g., deterministic interval quantizers or location families), in which case we state it for those classes and support the general case empirically. (3) AQ-UCB's clipped-window estimator is biased for non-sub-Gaussian rewards; controlling the bias-variance trade-off inside a confidence-bound analysis may require extra assumptions or a slightly loose log factor. (4) The real-data granularity experiment relies on replayed/off-policy logs and simulated annotator noise, so it is indicative rather than conclusive; multi-annotator noise estimates may be unreliable. (5) The bit-budget model idealizes measurement cost as uniform in bits, whereas human annotation cost may scale non-linearly with scale granularity; we will report sensitivity to alternative cost functions.

## 3. Whose Scale Is It Anyway? Monotone-Robust Bandits with Utility Elicitation

### Name

scalefree_bandits

### Short Hypothesis

Almost every deployed bandit optimizes the mean of an arbitrarily chosen numerical encoding of an ordinal signal (1-5 stars, 1-10 judge scores, clinical grades), yet the identity of the best arm is *not* invariant to monotone re-encodings of that signal; we hypothesize (i) such rank reversals and even non-transitive dominance cycles are common in real logged feedback, (ii) no algorithm that only samples rewards can resolve them, so a scale-free learner must play a randomized minimax-regret mixture over arms, and (iii) a handful of cheap utility-elicitation queries (O(L log 1/eps) standard-gamble comparisons over the L observation levels, independent of the number of arms and the horizon) suffices to recover near-optimal single-arm performance.

### Related Work

(1) Preference-based / ordinal bandits: Busa-Fekete et al. (preference-based racing, dueling bandits) and Borda/Copeland dueling-bandit work (Yue & Joachims; Zoghi et al.; Urvoy et al.) study rank-based targets, but they assume the *comparison* relation is given as the feedback channel and adopt a single fixed scale-free target (Borda, Copeland, von Neumann winner) as the definition of optimality. We instead keep standard cardinal bandit feedback, treat the utility as an unknown nuisance object in a monotone class U, define regret as worst-case over U, and show that the choice among Borda/Copeland/mean is itself a modelling assumption that our minimax formulation dispenses with. (2) Risk-aware bandits (CVaR, mean-variance, distributional/quantile bandits) replace the mean with a different fixed functional; they are not scale-free and do not consider sets of utilities. Stochastic-dominance-based selection has appeared in racing algorithms, but only as a partial-order screening rule, with no minimax-regret objective, no randomization result, and no elicitation. (3) Imprecise-reward sequential decision making with active elicitation (Regan & Boutilier, minimax-regret MDPs; Weng & Zanuttini; active preference elicitation for reward learning; RLHF reward modelling) assumes access to the environment model or focuses on offline reward learning; we couple elicitation with *online exploration under bandit feedback* and give a sample/query complexity split. (4) Reward-scale sensitivity of LLM-as-judge leaderboards has been noted empirically but never framed as an identifiability problem in sequential decision making with matching upper and lower bounds. Our contributions -- an unavoidability result driven by non-transitive-dice constructions, a game-theoretic randomized minimax-regret algorithm operating on CDF confidence bands, an elicitation-complexity theorem, and a large empirical audit of real ordinal feedback -- are new.

### Abstract

Bandit algorithms maximize the mean of a number, but in most applications that number is an arbitrary encoding of an ordinal signal: a five-star rating, a 1-10 LLM-judge score, a clinical severity grade. Mapping stars to utilities as 1,2,3,4,5 rather than 1,2,3,4,10 is a modelling choice that no one validates, and it can flip which arm is best. We formalize the monotone-robust bandit: rewards take values in an ordered set of L levels, the true utility u is an unknown increasing function, and the learner is judged by regret maximized over all utilities consistent with what it knows. First, we characterize identifiability: an arm is optimal for every utility exactly when it first-order stochastically dominates the others, and we show, via non-transitive-dice constructions, that the pairwise majority relation among arm distributions can cycle. Consequently any algorithm that only samples rewards -- however many -- must incur linear worst-case regret if it commits to a single arm, whereas a randomized mixture provably attains a strictly smaller minimax regret given by the value of a zero-sum game between the learner and the utility. We give MR-UCB, which maintains DKW-style CDF confidence bands, solves the induced robust game by a linear program, and plays the maximin mixture; it attains sublinear regret with respect to the game value, with rates governed by a dominance gap rather than a mean gap. Second, we add a second feedback channel: standard-gamble queries about the utility scale. We prove O(L log(1/eps)) queries -- independent of the number of arms and horizon -- suffice for eps-max-regret, give a query lower bound, and a max-regret-reduction query rule. Finally, we audit MovieLens, Amazon reviews, Yelp and public LLM-judge scores, quantifying how often mean-based rankings and leaderboards are scale artifacts.

### Experiments

1) Scale-fragility audit of real ordinal feedback (no training needed, cheap). Datasets: MovieLens-25M (1-5 stars), Amazon Reviews subsets, Yelp reviews, Open Bandit Dataset (binary, as a control), and public LLM-as-judge score sets (e.g., MT-Bench / AlpacaEval-style 1-10 or Likert judgments). For items/arms with >= n_min observations: (a) fraction of pairs with crossing CDFs (i.e., no first-order dominance) among pairs whose mean gap is 'significant'; (b) reversal rate: fraction of pairs whose mean order flips under some monotone re-encoding of the L levels (an LP feasibility check over the monotone utility polytope) and under a natural family (CRRA/power transforms u(x)=x^gamma, gamma in [0.2,5]); (c) top-arm robustness: is the mean-argmax item FOSD-undominated? how often does the argmax change over gamma? (d) non-transitivity: rate of 3-cycles in the relation P(X_a > X_b) > 1/2 (ties split), and in the mean relation under a common but unknown u. Metric headline: percentage of leaderboard/top-10 rankings that are scale artifacts.
2) Synthetic validation of the theory. K in {3,5,20} arms with L=5 or L=10 level distributions, including a hand-built non-transitive-dice instance and instances sampled to match empirical MovieLens rating shapes; T up to 1e5. Algorithms: (i) UCB on the naive 1..L encoding; (ii) UCB on a mis-specified encoding (power transform); (iii) Copeland/Borda-style dueling-bandit baseline run on simulated pairwise comparisons of sampled rewards; (iv) FOSD screening + uniform play on the undominated set; (v) proposed MR-UCB: per-arm empirical CDFs with simultaneous DKW confidence bands, then solve max_{p in Delta_K} min_{u in U_hat} [ sum_a p_a V_u(a) - max_b V_u(b) ] as an LP (finite L makes U a polytope, V_u linear in u), play p_t, plus optimism by using the most favourable CDF in the band. Metrics: cumulative *robust* regret max_{u in U} regret_u; regret under a hidden ground-truth u; regret under the naive mean (to show what is given up); convergence of the estimated game value to the true value.
3) Randomization gap. On the non-transitive instance and on real-data-fitted triples, compute the exact minimax regret of the best single arm vs. the best mixture; report the gap and verify that MR-UCB's realized robust regret tracks the mixture value while any deterministic rule is linear in T. Grid-search over deterministic policies to confirm tightness.
4) Elicitation complexity. Add a query oracle answering comparisons between two lotteries over levels ('is a sure 3 better than a 50/50 of 1 and 5?'), which is a halfspace cut on the utility polytope. Compare query rules: random cuts, center-of-mass/ellipsoid halving, and our max-regret-reduction rule (query the cut that most reduces current minimax regret, following the current-solution heuristic). Plot achieved max-regret and true-utility regret vs. query budget Q for L in {5,7,10} and K in {5,20,100}; verify the predicted O(L log(1/eps)) scaling and the predicted independence from K and T. Noisy oracle variant: logistic answer noise with repetition; measure robustness and cost inflation.
5) Joint exploration-elicitation trade-off. Fix a combined budget in which one query costs c samples, c in {1,10,100,1000}; compare an interleaved scheduler (query only when the current robust game value exceeds the achievable reduction) against sample-only and query-first baselines. Metric: true-utility cumulative regret at fixed budget.
6) LLM-evaluation case study. Treat K response generators as arms and public judge scores as ordinal rewards; run MR-UCB and mean-UCB in replay. Report how many of the top-5 rankings are FOSD-justified, and how few utility queries (answered by a fixed simulated 'principal' utility, or by a second LLM acting as the principal) are needed to make the ranking scale-robust.

### Risk Factors and Limitations

(1) First-order stochastic dominance and Borda/Copeland targets already exist in the bandit literature; the novelty rests on the minimax-regret-over-utilities objective, the randomization result, and elicitation complexity. If a prior work already couples elicitation with bandit exploration, the contribution narrows to the empirical audit and the game-theoretic algorithm. (2) The audit's conclusions depend on the monotone class U: with fully unconstrained monotone utilities, non-comparability may be so pervasive that results look vacuous; we will therefore report a graded family of constraint sets (unconstrained monotone, bounded-curvature, CRRA) and treat the amount of structure needed for identifiability as a finding rather than an assumption. (3) DKW-band-based confidence sets give conservative constants; instance-dependent optimality may require more delicate analysis, and our regret bound may be off by log factors from a matching lower bound. (4) Real logged ratings are heavily confounded (self-selection, popularity bias), so reversal rates measure the fragility of the observed distributions rather than causal effects; we will note this and include a bias-robustness check on subsets with dense observations. (5) The elicitation oracle assumes a coherent expected-utility principal; violations (framing effects, non-EU preferences) would invalidate the standard-gamble cuts, though the sampling-only minimax results remain valid. (6) Extending to continuous rewards or contextual/linear settings requires discretization and is only partially addressed.
